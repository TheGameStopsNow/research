# The parts of GME's options chain that never trade, and the machines that do

**TL;DR:** Twice this spring, 10,000 calls and 10,000 puts printed on GME at the same millisecond and then froze solid. On 817 different days, open interest has appeared on this chain with no trades behind it: 670,322 contracts, booked silently, accumulating fastest in the quietest weeks. An entire December 2027 expiration was born the morning after GameStop priced the first of $4.2 billion in zero-percent converts. And a large position appeared at the $32 warrant strike weeks before the warrant was announced, through a channel that leaves no prints. The largest call position open in the name today, 100,000 contracts at that same $32 strike, is not on the options chain at all.

The "this is how they control the price" stories do not hold up either. Gamma hedging, price magnets, pinning, IV suppression, and baited flow each got a pre-registered test, and each one died. What survived instead: married pairs that behave like loans, not bets; a silent bookkeeping ledger; a lottery-ticket ritual that ran 110,332 tiny tickets like clockwork for eight months and then went dark; a writing machine at $50 whose entire edge is that the squeeze never comes on its clock; and exactly one signal on the visible tape that carries forward information. Everything below is measured, dated, and replicable, and it ends with the specific events that would break each finding.

> **Position disclosure:** I hold a long position in GME. I am not a financial advisor, attorney, or affiliated with any entity named in this post.

## Q1: Who buys 10,000 calls AND 10,000 puts at the exact same millisecond?

Twice this spring, in GME's far-dated book, it happened: 10,000 calls and 10,000 puts, same strike, same expiration, printed at the same millisecond. April 14th at the Dec-2026 $37 strike. May 7th at $50. Exchange complex books marry spread legs within a single order, so no spread order can produce this print. Two enormous legs, coordinated to the millisecond.

Annotations give each giant's clip count and largest single print, which is the distinction that matters: July 2019's 18,400 at top left is seven clips laddered on one strike, while the $37 unit is a single 10,000-lot ticket. Panel B redraws every event as a line from its print date to its expiration. Almost all are short stubs expiring within weeks. Nine events run past 200 days and five reach into 2027, so the long lines at the right are not just the two giants: the two 10,000-lot units are only the heaviest of them, and the longest-dated of all are the smaller $30 rehearsal pairs into Jan-27. Nothing in the census runs beyond 358 days.](figures/fig01_married_census.png)

That's every one this detector finds in GME's *listed* series: **406 married clips rolling up into 278 events since February 2018**. Take that as a floor, not a total. It counts only what printed, only in series the public tape carries, only at 250 contracts a side or more, only when the two legs landed within two seconds of each other, and only since February 2018. Q4 will show that positions on this chain can appear with no print behind them at all. Every "largest" and "first" below is bounded by that census, not by the world. There is also a second, bespoke chain that no screener shows, carrying larger pairs than these; Q3 is that chain.

Size alone is not new. July 2019 stacked 18,400 and 15,425 contracts per side at the $8 strike in the pre-squeeze borrow build-up, though those were ladders rather than single tickets: seven and fourteen same-millisecond clips on one strike, the largest single print among them 7,500. And one single 10,000-lot married print does predate this spring: **June 7, 2024, at $40, seven days from expiry.**

So the size has precedent and the tenor has precedent, but never together. Every prior 10,000-lot married print was days from expiry: seven days in June 2024, nine and ten in July 2019. Long tenors exist too, and they are small: the longest-dated events before this year are 500-odd contracts reaching into 2022. These two units are **248 and 253 days out at 10,000 contracts a side**, roughly thirty-five times the tenor at the same size, and no earlier event in the census combines the two. No event in the census runs beyond 358 days.

And they were not the first attempts at that date. **Four married events target January 15, 2027, and the $50 unit is the last of them.** Before it: $30 on January 22 and again on February 2, then $23 on March 19: 2,000, 3,000 and 1,100 contracts, built from 1,000- and 1,500-lot clips. Two of those carry *longer* tenors at inception than the unit they preceded. Rehearsals on the same expiration, growing toward it.

A long call plus a short put at the same strike is, economically, a share of stock with no stock. Traders call it a synthetic forward, and it's the building block of the *conversion/reversal*, the machinery desks use to finance share positions and manufacture or cover borrow. The stock can rise or fall without changing what the combination is for. It's a loan, wearing options.

## Q2: If it's a loan, where's the proof?

Three places.

**The puts are frozen.** Since the day each unit printed, its put leg has sat still: 10,045 contracts on unit 1, unmoved for 66 straight sessions; 10,011 on unit 2. No trades, no exercises, even though these puts spent weeks $13–29 *in the money*, where a normal holder exercises or trades out. Meanwhile, the same chain's "wild" deep-ITM puts (same strikes, outside the structure) were exercised down to zero. Whoever is inside these positions does not want optionality. They want whatever the position *carries*.

![Reading the chart: top, daily put open interest in the two unit legs (red, orange). Each jumps from near zero to ~10,000 on its print day (dashed verticals) and then draws a flat line for months: no trades, no exercises. The control behavior is visible in the same panel: the $50 strike's pre-existing "wild" puts (the early orange series) were exercised away to zero in late March, which is what normal deep-ITM holders do. Bottom, spot against the two strikes: the vertical gap is the puts' in-the-money depth, and the shaded weeks are the stretch where the frozen legs sat $13–29 in the money and still never moved.](figures/fig03_frozen_puts.png)

**It prices like a loan.** You can back the financing rate straight out of the married prices (discounted put-call parity). That reading is cleaner here than it would be almost anywhere else: a parity gap has only two possible sources, a borrow cost or a dividend expectation, and GameStop has paid no dividend since 2019. Net out the risk-free rate and what is left can only be the cost of borrowing shares. During the 2020 hard-borrow era, these combos priced an implied borrow of roughly **+19% per year**, which is what a desperate borrow market looked like from the inside. The two new units price at +2.3% and +5.5%, cheap money in a calm era. And in 2023, AMC ran the same machine at **+189–235% per year** during its worst borrow stress: one 42,567-contract-per-side monster, assembled over two days, born three weeks after the APE settlement term sheet and expiring the day the court rejected that settlement's first version (July 21, 2023; a revised version was approved three weeks later and APE converted the week after). The machine shows up where financing is painful, and its prices track the pain.

Right, the same rates against an independent same-day borrow estimate: in the calm era the two agree at ρ = 0.85, which is how a real financing rate should behave.](figures/fig02_implied_borrow.png)

**Other names run the same machine.** The married-pair signature shows up in 10 of the 12 names I tested, and where it can be dated against stress, it clusters in the exact windows when each stock was hard to borrow. AMC in 2023. COIN through its own convert, stablecoin-collapse, exchange-failure and SEC calendar. GME now. BYND clusters too. The hosts run in parallel, each on its own stress calendar, with no hand-offs between them. The far-dated 10,000-lot serial units, though, are only here.

## Q3: What if the biggest position in this name isn't on the chain at all?

Everything above came off the listed options chain: the series the exchanges publish, that every screener and every data vendor carries. There is a second chain, and nothing on it reaches a screener.

FLEX options are exchange-traded and cleared by the OCC like any other option, but their terms are negotiated rather than listed: any strike, any expiration date, either exercise style. Because the terms are bespoke, each contract gets its own series, and those series are not disseminated on the public options tape. They are absent from OPRA, and therefore absent from the per-contract feed behind every number in this post. The OCC publishes them separately, daily and free, in a fixed-width text file. One scope note before any of it counts. My FLEX archive begins **11 March 2024**, and GameStop has no FLEX open interest anywhere in it until **6 January 2025**: not a gap, but zero GME rows in all 205 daily reports I hold from 2024, contiguous apart from market holidays. What came before March 2024 I cannot see, and neither can anyone else now, so read this as a book that is nineteen months old rather than one that provably never existed earlier.

Read that file for GME and **the largest call position open in the name is not on the chain: 100,000 contracts of the $32 call expiring January 2028.** Ten million shares of exposure in one line, American style. As of Friday 24 July 2026, that single bespoke line is **twice the biggest listed call line in the name**: 49,883 contracts at $50, expiring January 2027. Two bounds on that, both worth saying out loud. It is not the largest the listed chain has ever carried; 180,319 contracts sat at $127.50 in January 2024 and 169,963 at $20 that June, so this is the largest position open now, not the largest ever written. And it is not far clear of this year's listed peak either: the $30 line expiring June 2026 reached 99,503 contracts on 6 May, within half a percent of it. So the thing worth noticing was never that this is the biggest number on the board. It is that it is the biggest one nobody can see. It stands at 80,000 on the first report of January 2026, reaches 100,000 by early February, and has not moved since, still 100,000 as of last Friday. **All 100,000 of them are invisible**, and the reason is a single day on the calendar. GameStop's listed January-2028 series expires Friday the 21st, the third Friday, like every standard series. This one expires **Thursday the 20th**, a date on which GameStop has no listed option at all. There is no listed twin for it to be compared against, netted against, or converted into. (The listed $32 series one day later is a separate line carrying 12,916 contracts as of 24 July 2026; it is a different position, not the visible remainder of this one.) That one-day step is not a rounding artifact of my reader. It is the same maneuver as the penny strikes below, made on the calendar instead of the strike ladder, and it is the cleanest example in this post of a position built to stay in a series of its own. Hold the strike in mind; the warrant lands on it in Q6.

The married signature runs here too, larger and earlier. FLEX carries call-and-put pairs at identical strike, identical expiration and identical open interest: the synthetic shape from Q1, in a venue Q1 cannot see. The largest is **30,000 contracts per side at $25, expiring July 2025**, with another at 14,621 per side at $23 that September. So the census above needs its scope said out loud: 406 clips and 278 events cover **listed series only.** Across both chains I can see, this spring's 10,000-lot units are neither the largest of their kind nor the first.

Then the detail that changes the reading, because it is a design choice rather than a behavior. **The FLEX pairs are European style, all of them.** Across the whole retained history there are 24 matched call/put pairs in GME's FLEX book, and the split by exercise style is **24 European to 0 American**. Q2 argued from conduct that whoever sits inside these structures does not want optionality: the put legs spent months $13–29 in the money and were never exercised. In the bespoke book the same shape is bought in a contract where early exercise is *impossible*. The listed chain shows someone declining to exercise. This one shows someone buying an instrument that cannot be.

And the split is not a property of the venue, because the same book contains the opposite choice. **The 100,000-contract $32 call is American.** Whoever holds it can exercise any number of contracts on any business day, 1 or 100,000, with no public notice until the next morning's report. Both instruments were bought in a venue where either style was available, by parties who specified every term. The carry structures were built so that exercise cannot happen; the single large call was built so that it can.

Which makes the next fact the important one: **it never has.** The line appears at 80,000 in December 2025, steps once to 100,000 in January 2026, and then holds at exactly 100,000 for **137 of the 160 report-days I have**: two distinct values across its entire life, the second of them unchanged for seven months. Ten million shares of delivery, available on demand, and the option has sat untouched through two earnings prints, a warrant distribution and a 22% single-day drawdown. The capability is real. The discretion is real. Neither has been used.

And they are struck *at the money*. Across all 24 FLEX synthetic pair-lines the median strike sits **3.6% from spot on the day the line first appears**, against 13.7% for a null that keeps the same strikes and the same dates but shuffles which goes with which (p = 0.0001); newly struck lines sit closer to spot than lines already alive on that same day (p = 0.009), so it is not an artifact of the stock drifting. Of the handoffs where the strike actually changes, both land within 1.3% of spot. This is the cheapest possible way to hold a share-equivalent: a call and a put at the same at-the-money strike is a synthetic forward, carrying delta near one for almost no net premium. It is what you do to *carry* exposure, not to bet on direction, the same conclusion Q2 reaches from the parity rate, reached again from the geometry of the strikes alone.

One more pattern, offered as an inference about mechanics and not about anyone's intent. Many of these FLEX strikes are offset by exactly one cent: $22.010, $23.010, $24.010, $30.010. FLEX allows arbitrary strikes, so a penny costs nothing economically. What it buys is non-fungibility. A FLEX series whose terms match a listed series becomes fungible with it, and the OCC then converts the open interest into the listed line, which subjects it to the tighter position limits that govern standard options. A one-cent offset keeps a position bespoke, in its own series, and outside those limits. That is what the offset accomplishes. Whether it is why anyone chose it, this data cannot say.

And then, in March 2026, the pair engine stopped. The European book peaks at **47,536 contracts in February 2026**, holds 33,768 into early March, and on **20 March** the 15,000-per-side $25 pair, the largest pair GME's FLEX book ever carried at that point, simply expired. It did not roll. The largest pair written afterwards is 1,435 per side, an order of magnitude smaller. By April the European book is 4,810, by June 3,145, and as of last Friday **2,495 contracts, a 95% collapse that has not reversed in four months.** Through the whole shutdown the American $32 call never moved a contract. Two structures, one book, opposite behavior: the carry machine wound down and the static position stayed exactly static. Call that an observation, not a finding. Four months of silence is suggestive and nothing more, and a pair engine that restarts at size kills the reading above.

That same conversion rule is an objection to the section that follows, so I tested it first. If the OCC converts FLEX open interest into listed series administratively, then listed open interest can appear with no trade behind it, which is precisely the finding in Q4. Two things rule it out as the driver. **Capacity:** total GME FLEX open interest peaks at 150,766 contracts, against 670,322 contracts of cumulative unattributed creation in the listed book, and conversion would have to *drain* FLEX by the latter figure. **Conservation:** if conversion drove those builds, FLEX open interest would fall on the days listed open interest rose. Across the 145 overlapping days the rank correlation is **+0.038 (p = 0.65)**, the wrong sign, and not significant.

One deflation before this section gets more weight than it earns. None of this is exotic *as a venue*. Matched FLEX call/put pairs appear on every large root in the file, on all 596 report-days I hold: NVDA peaks at 3.47 million contracts of FLEX open interest, MSFT at 2.39 million, SPY at 2.32 million, against GameStop's 150,766. On that scale GME's bespoke book is a rounding error, and "this name has FLEX positions" is not a finding; it is a description of how the FLEX market works everywhere. Nor is a large American line rare on its own: 143 of them clear 50,000 contracts across all roots in this window. What is unusual is narrower and worth stating precisely. Most of those large American lines come in laddered sets: MSFT's are 100,000 / 200,000 / 100,000 at three strikes on one expiration, the signature of a collar or a call spread. GameStop's is a lone strike, unpaired, unmoving, in a series whose expiration date exists nowhere else in the name. That is the distinctive part, and it is a claim about *composition*, not about size or secrecy.

The honest limits are narrow but real. This report carries open interest only: no prints, no volume, no timestamps, so I can see a position's size on a report day and not the trades that built it. Counterparty is invisible here exactly as it is everywhere else. And unlike the rest of this post, the FLEX inventory is a description drawn from a public file plus the falsification tests above, not a pre-registered forward test. The daily history behind it is my own archive plus every report OCC still serves. The agency's window runs roughly twenty-four months, and its floor sat at the end of July 2024 when I last checked, so this archive reaches about four months further back than anything a reader starting today could fetch. That floor moves forward every day.

## Q4: What if a big share of the "positions" on this chain never traded at all?

This one sounds broken. Open interest is supposed to change when contracts trade. But in GME's deep-put far book, open interest *appears*, new positions booked overnight, with no corresponding trades on any exchange, at **6.3× the rate of all attributable order flow** (12.6× in the deepest corner). All told: **670,322 contracts** across 817 days, arriving off-tape.

I spent weeks trying to kill this. Coverage gaps in the data? No; it doesn't grow on thin-data days. A schema bug? That objection is the strongest one, and I have since found two bugs in the reader this project uses for the per-print tape: it names the expiration column two ways and encodes calls and puts two ways, and both drop data without raising an error. A dropped print is precisely what would manufacture this result. So it matters that this comparison never touches that reader. The open interest comes from the daily clearing snapshot and the volume it is measured against comes from the end-of-day options file, and across all 7,010 files in those two stores there is exactly one column naming and one call/put encoding, with nothing unreadable. Backfills, restatements, corporate-action adjustments? Excluded one by one. FLEX conversion, the one route that creates listed open interest administratively? Excluded on capacity and on sign, in Q3. What's left is real: positions created off-tape, by settlement bookkeeping; not exercise (the new positions are months-to-years from expiry, 99.3% in far-dated monthly lines), and not on any clock you can set a watch by. The candidates that remain live in the clearing layer: position transfers between clearing members, account-to-account moves, adjustments booked after the fact at the clearinghouse. Three frozen sharpening tests narrowed it further: the births do not ride the series-listing calendar (they book into lines that already exist, at exactly their coverage share), they are not a rebooking artifact of the October warrant adjustment, and exercise is excluded on both rights. What survives classification is transfer-and-rebooking-class settlement bookkeeping. Which member or account it belongs to, the public tape cannot say, and I won't guess.

And then the strange part: **it's growing.** Slow, monotonic, and independent of attention. The accumulation runs fastest in the quietest weeks.

One more thread to pull: the silent builds cluster around two kinds of days: the days the married financing units print, and the day *after* earnings. And they land away from the financing strikes, deep in the cemetery of dead strikes: counterparty settlement, filed where no screener points. That hypothesis is registered for forward testing; it lives or dies on the next unit and the next two earnings prints.

## Q5: What was born on the day GameStop borrowed $4.2 billion?

In the spring of 2025, GameStop itself went borrowing, and the way it did it left footprints all over this chain. In March the company sold **$1.3 billion** of convertible notes ($1.5 billion once the purchasers' option was exercised); in June, **$2.25 billion** more ($2.7 billion with its option), both at zero percent interest. A convertible note is a bond that can turn into shares at a fixed price (here, about $29.85 and $28.91), and the desks that buy these deals run a mechanical trade: buy the note, short the stock against it, hedge the rest with options. That machinery is why pricing days hurt. On **March 27**, the day the first note priced, the stock fell **22.1%**. On **June 12**, the day the second priced, it fell **22.5%**. You are watching the hedge flow arrive, twice, on schedule.

The options chain has a birthday that matches. On the morning of **March 28, 2025** (the snapshot that records positions from the March 27 session, the first pricing day), the chain gained an entire expiration out of nowhere: **December 17, 2027**, 994 days out. Eighteen lines at once, calls from $10 to $55, puts from $3 to $35, **34,083 contracts** already resting in them. (Those eighteen are the lines this test tracks. Count every line in the raw clearing file carrying any open interest that morning and you get 33 lines holding 34,194, the extra fifteen splitting 111 contracts between them.) There is no other day like it in the whole pre-reset era: the largest first-appearance day in that window, 4.3× the runner-up, in a period where new far-dated lines carried positions at birth on 3 of 91 days.

And the shape of the seed is the story. **93% of the new far-dated mass sat in one line: the $5 put**, 31,721 contracts on day one. Over its life that line became the largest off-tape cell in the book: 193,804 of its contracts arrived through the silent channel from Q4. The same morning, one more line opened at the same expiry: **the $32 call, at 3 contracts**. Keep that line in mind; the warrant later landed on it.

![Reading the chart: panel A is the December 2027 expiration on its first morning, eighteen lines born with 34,083 contracts already resting and 93% of the mass in the $5 put; the green verticals mark the two conversion prices. Panel B tracks the $5 put (red) and $32 call (blue) growing in steps that land on the notes' pricing and closing days. Panel C is the stock on those same days, down 22% twice, on schedule.](figures/fig16_convert_shadow.png)

Then the confirmation arrived on schedule. In June the $5 put line sat flat at 43,428 contracts for weeks. On **June 12, the second pricing day**, it jumped **+19,131** in a single step, the second-largest in its history after the seed itself, with a further step on June 17 when the deal closed. No exchange prints either time; the growth came through the silent channel. Both notes, same pattern: this structure grows on the days the deals price and close. One more oddity that morning: 21,510 contracts of the **$55 call expiring that very day**, at two and a half times the stock price. By shape, gap insurance around the pricing window.

Two honesty rails before this becomes a story it isn't. First, I cannot see the side: open interest is unsigned, so the $5 put seed could be someone buying disaster insurance or someone booking financing inventory; the tape records that the book was seeded, not which side of the counterparty's ledger it sits on. Second, a $5 put is not a convert-hedge strike: the notes convert near $29, and a textbook convert desk hedges with short stock plus options near the conversion price, not puts 80% out of the money. The clean sentence is this: the convert calendar explains *when* the book was born, the silent channel explains *how* it kept growing, and what grew inside it is the financing geography you've already met. The two facts compose rather than compete.

The December 2027 book, the cemetery where the silent ledger lives, was seeded on the convert calendar, grew through the silent channel, and six months later the warrant landed on it. One book, three chapters. This was the first.

## Q6: What was sitting at the warrant strike before the warrants existed?

On October 7, 2025, GameStop distributed warrants to its shareholders: call options issued by the company itself, strike **$32**, expiring October 30, 2026, one warrant for every ten shares. The terms were announced on September 9, 2025. The distribution reached the convert book too: holders of the 2030 and 2032 notes received warrants on an as-converted basis (3.34970 and 3.45872 per $1,000 of face), which ties the two borrowing chapters to the $32 line by contract, in writing.

On August 6 and August 14, 2025, three to five weeks *before* that announcement, **4,376 and 3,769 contracts appeared** in a single line: the December 2027 LEAPS $32 call. Open interest there went 1,048 → 5,320 → 9,139. Prints in that line on those days: **zero**. Total volume across the whole strike on each day covered less than a tenth of the build. The position arrived through the same off-tape channel as Q4, at the exact strike the company was about to print on the warrant, before the warrant publicly existed.

The boring explanation goes first, because it deserves to: banks build warrant distributions, and the banks working on this one knew the terms before September 9. A dealer pre-positioning around a deal it has been mandated on counts as ordinary deal mechanics rather than a leak. If that's all this was, three things still need explaining: why the position took the silent channel instead of the tape, why it chose a December 2027 LEAPS line rather than paper near the warrant's own October 2026 expiry, and why it died with the old chain instead of re-forming. The mundane story covers the *when*. It has much less to say about the *how* and the *where*.

I ran the fingerprint checks anyway. It is not the financing program (no married pairs, no put leg; puts at $32 sat flat near 1,900). There is no crowd texture to match, because the births left no prints; the visible tape around those weeks was the ordinary small-lot crowd, flipping to generic on announcement day. And the specificity is real: the loading at $32 in that window was the top of all 16 strikes tested.

Two limits on how hard that can be pushed. Reporting the largest of sixteen strikes is not the same as a significance test, and I have no honest p-value for the proposition that the company later chose this particular strike. And I have not shown that $32 was unguessable: if the warrant strike was derivable from public information, a round number above a conversion price near $29, or a standard premium to the share price at the time, then a desk positioning there needed nothing private and the timing stops being remarkable. It is specific and it is dated. I cannot price its improbability.

The epilogue is as strange as the birth. When the October reset wiped the old chain (on the warrant's October 7 ex-date, the OCC re-adjusted every listed GME contract; the old lines retired and the book re-grew in fresh paper), $32 survived better than every other strike I tested; but the precursor's own LEAPS line did not re-form (9,191 → 0 → about 600 by November). The post-reset wall at $32 (real, permanent, sitting in the warrant's expiry window) grew in new paper. The old position died with the old chain.

What the tape lets me say: a large, specific, off-tape position appeared at the warrant strike weeks before the warrant was announced, through the same silent channel as Q4. What it doesn't say: who placed it, or what they knew. The tape answers the first kind of question. It is silent on the second, and I will not fill that silence with a story.

## Q7: Who buys 110,000 lottery tickets, always before 10 a.m.?

Between October 2025 and June 2026, a single, unmistakable species of trade lived on this chain: a 1-contract, $10-wide, deep-out-of-the-money call vertical, about 91 days out, costing **$0.11 for $10 of width**. 110,332 tickets. 127,875 contracts. $1.86 million of premium. 98.8% single contracts. Born before 10 a.m., closed by early afternoon, same venue, same shape, day after day: a little round-trip treadmill.

Its biography is the best part, and it starts well before the census window above. The shape was born on **May 16, 2024, three days after the Kitty return** (Keith Gill's reappearance), in the attention flood. The eight months of clockwork are the finished ritual; the seventeen months before them are the assembly, trait by trait: the 1-lot rule locked in June 2025, the pre-10am clock in March 2026, the death clock drifting earlier and earlier until it parked at noon, the market's midday lull. And then, on **June 24, 2026**, it stopped. Seventeen straight sessions of silence. Whatever ran this ritual finished it.

## Q8: What's the machine at $50?

If you've followed the $50 strike, you know the mystery going in: a wall of calls appeared there on **January 27, 2021**, and in some form it has never left, with a low put wall kept underneath it the whole time. I wrote about that persistence myself, and the full five-year census now confirms the facts while correcting two details from my old thread. The birth was real and overnight: from zero to 33,026 contracts in one session, which is 132,104 in post-split units, the "~132,000" I quoted then. But it was born at the **$200 strike**; it only became the $50 after the 4:1 split. (The literal $50 that day was a different, deep-in-the-money line, and it *shrank* that week.) Since then the strike has been occupied every single day: 1,359 of 1,359, bottoming at 977 contracts in the April 2024 lull, peaking at 121,993 in June 2026. The put floor is real too; deep puts have outnumbered the call wall on 100% of those days.

What's new is the mechanism, and it runs on a calendar, not a brain. My old thread described the wall as "rolled" forward through 83 expiries. The census disagrees on both the count and the verb. The wall has lived **173** position-scale lives (83 matches no natural counting rule), and they do not roll. A huge cohort accumulates for months, is held flat into expiration, and then (this surprised me) it dies: less than 21% of the dying open interest reappears in the next line, and what cross-expiry switching exists runs *below chance*. Of the 40 cohorts that died at size (10,000+ contracts), **35 reincarnated, exactly 1 rolled** (March 2022), and 1 rebuilt slowly over a quarter. Days after each death a brand-new cohort starts rebuilding from fresh prints, ~200–300 contracts a day, keyed to the previous cohort's death. The strike never empties, but no position survives it: **the $50 line reincarnates; it doesn't roll**, and it has worked this way since April 2021, uninterrupted at scale since March 2022. So consider this a public correction to my old thread: not one position rolled forward eighty-some times, but 173 serial reincarnations of the same strike.

And the economics are not what "smart money" is supposed to look like. One tracked cohort farmed **$986.5k of premium over 64 days** and ate **$842.5k of adverse revaluation on just the 4 worst days**. Two qualifiers. First, that cohort is the thinnest of the four I could measure: coverage ran 8.6× in the January 2026 cycle and 2.9× in June, so "thin" describes this cycle rather than the machine's whole history. Second, those are naked numbers: a delta-hedged book has most of that revaluation offset by the hedge, and the comparison loses its force. So the honest reading is conditional. Unhedged, this is a book collecting thin premium while short the exact tail this stock has delivered twice, and its edge is that the squeeze never arrives on its cycle. Hedged, it is ordinary inventory and these economics say little. I cannot settle which: the prints carry no stock ties, and the public swap tape, all 367 days of it, shows nothing at its scale.

## Q9: Why is $45 empty?

Between the lottery crowd below and the $50 machine above sits a strike almost nobody will hold: **$45 has 2,569 contracts resting against $40's 23.8k and $50's 63.9k**, about 15× emptier than chance for a round strike in that spot. Read the chart as flow (contracts traded, not held) and the emptiest bar is actually $41, one strike right of the 40 tick, with $45 close behind. Both sit in the same dead zone, but with different textures: $41 barely trades at all, while $45 gets printed on and nothing stays. The $45 hole isn't a moneyness effect and it isn't a price ceiling; it's a fixed dollar line, born in the 2021 squeeze and re-dug at last October's reset, when both neighboring crowds rebuilt and this one didn't. The chain has a no-man's-land, and both crowds respect it.

## Q10: Why does the dead part of the chain twitch all day?

Zoom into the strikes nobody trades and you'll see the quotes never sit still: sizes fluttering by ±1 contract, venues rotating, prices re-marking. It looks like someone watching on edge. It isn't. A venue-side auto-quoting engine drives it: no clock, no schedule, a mean-reverting size randomizer, and one remarkable reflex. When a real print hits the $50 line, the engine reacts in **one second** (91% within two), not by changing the price (that takes it a leisurely ~73 seconds) but by reshuffling display size between venues. The same engine family runs on AMC's dead strikes with different settings. It sees everything and thinks nothing. A lot of what gets screenshotted as "manipulation" in the dead zones is this: infrastructure, idling.

## Q11: If all of this exists, why doesn't it move the price?

I tested every channel by which this structure could push the stock: gamma, vanna, and charm hedging flows; price magnets at the big strikes; the pin as a weapon; IV suppression by the $50 writer; baited flow that herds the chasers. Each failed against a pre-registered null. The chasers *fade* aggressive flow instead of chasing it; you can't walk this crowd anywhere. Price ignited twice in five years, and both times the visible fuel was an attention event, not a positioning channel I could measure. But even that is too clean. On the public record, the stock was already rising in May 2024 before Keith Gill's return, and the posts that followed were prepared rather than spontaneous. What the tape lets me say is narrower than "attention, not positioning": none of the mechanical channels by which the standing structure could push price fired. Whether the attention itself was positioned, the tape cannot see, and I should not have written the sentence as if it could. The structure is real; the force isn't; the spark's origin is still open.

But one thing on the visible tape *does* carry forward information: when the chain's **OI-writing velocity** runs hot, implied volatility *falls* over the following one to four weeks. The effect is small, rank correlations around −0.1, which is why it takes a full census to see at all rather than a few memorable episodes.

That claim used to include option volume too, and the obvious objection is what removed it. Heavy writing happens when premium is rich, premium is rich when volatility and activity are already elevated, and elevated things come down on their own, so the whole result could have been mean reversion in a costume. The confound is real and I measured it: writing velocity tracks the current IV level at +0.18 and the current volume level at +0.38, while both levels predict their own forward decline at −0.22 to −0.39. So I residualized the signal against each target's own level and its trailing 21-session path. **The volume leg does not survive.** It falls from −0.08 and −0.13 to +0.03 and +0.01, insignificant and sign-flipped, and I am retiring it from the forward scorer. **The IV leg does survive**, at −0.069 and −0.068 with p below 0.002, and in the post-2021 era it barely attenuates at all: −0.106 and −0.122 against raw values of −0.104 and −0.125.

So half of my one surviving signal was the confound, and what is left is narrower than what I started with: writing velocity carries information about forward implied volatility, and nothing I can demonstrate about forward volume. That half is registered as a formal forward prediction, frozen in advance, scoring weekly. It may still die. That's the deal.

The scoring is live as of this writing. The specification froze on 22 July 2026, and the first bankable week closed on Friday 24 July with the chain's writing velocity at **+19,682 contracts**. That is a hot reading, so the standing prediction is that implied volatility falls into late August. The 21-day target matures on **21 August 2026**, and the first review comes after twelve scored weeks, around **16 October**. I am publishing the reading before the outcome, which is the only order in which a claim like this one means anything.

## Q12: What would break these readings?

Every finding above has a specific event that would kill it, and most of those events are already on the calendar:

- **A third married unit.** The program printed 10k pairs at $37 and $50. If a third ~10,000-lot married pair appears at **$40 or $45** in the Dec-26/Jan-27 book, the serial program is confirmed to be marching up the ladder. I run a watch on it every weeknight.
- **Expiry day.** If the frozen put legs expire untouched (**Dec 18, 2026 and Jan 15, 2027**), the held-to-maturity financing reading is confirmed. If they trade out early or exercise, it's dead.
- **The silent ledger.** If the next earnings print is *not* followed by an off-tape build the day after, the settlement-coupling hypothesis dies.
- **The next convert.** If GameStop prices another convertible note and the far-dated book shows no accretion on the pricing or closing days, the convert-shadow reading of the December 2027 book weakens. If it accretes again on schedule, the coupling hardens.
- **The warrant strike.** A new watch scores the $32 call wall every Saturday into the warrant's October 30 expiry. If the wall dies, if its warrant-expiry structure dissolves, or if the dead put side wakes up, the reading of that wall changes. And if anything books itself at $32 off-tape again, the watcher is already looking.
- **The one visible signal.** The forward scorer is running. The week of 20 July reads +19,682 contracts of writing velocity, which predicts falling implied volatility; the first target matures 21 August and the first review lands around 16 October. If the sign comes back wrong often enough across those twelve weeks, the last surviving signal in this post dies too, and I will report that here.

The dots, as I see them: this chain works less like a casino or a battlefield and more like a harbor. Financing ships dock where borrow is expensive, a fare-collector sits at $50, tides of bookkeeping run where nobody watches, and one weather vane points somewhere real. None of it pushes the price. All of it *explains* the price's weather.
